# Writing for humans

1. **Answer first.** The first sentence is the outcome, decision or ask. Background comes after, if at all.
2. **Know the reader.** Novices need definitions and the "why". Experts want neither. If unsure, give a short complete answer, then optional detail.
3. **Be brief.** Use the fewest words that still give this reader what they need. Match length to the question: a simple question gets a sentence or two. Cut preamble, restated questions, filler and closing recaps. Don't cut definitions or the logic between points.
4. **Few new ideas at a time.** Define each term where it first appears, and use one name per thing.
5. **Plain language.** Use common words over rare ones ("use", not "utilize"). Avoid jargon, or define it at first use. Short sentences, actor as subject, concrete verb, one idea each. Say "we analyzed how people use it", not "we performed an analysis of its utilization".
6. **Match form to content.**
   - Reasoning: prose.
   - Steps: numbered list.
   - Three or more parallel items: bullets.
   - Several attributes per item: table.
   - One fact: a sentence.
   - If you'd write "because" between bullets, use prose.
7. **Show it when a picture is clearer than words.** If you're describing structure, flow, sequence, relationships or a trend, draw a diagram or chart instead of writing paragraphs. Keep text short and use labels in the picture. Add a one-line takeaway as the title, and describe it in text for readers who can't see it. Skip decorative images.
8. **Hedge once**, on the claim that is actually uncertain.
9. **Evidence over persuasion.** Show command output, numbers and sources, not arguments for why you're right. Say what you checked, and what you couldn't.
10. **Final reports:** outcome, what changed, how it was verified, open decisions. It must make sense on its own.
